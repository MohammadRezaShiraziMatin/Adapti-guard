## 2 Related work
**Benchmarks.** AgentDojo provides an extensible environment for evaluating prompt-injection attacks and defenses in tool-using agents [2406.13352]. InjecAgent benchmarks indirect prompt injection in tool-integrated agents across user tools and attacker instructions [2403.02691]. BIPIA introduces a benchmark for indirect prompt injection [2312.14197]. LLMail-Inject reports a public adaptive prompt-injection challenge against an LLM-based email assistant [2506.09956]. LongPIBench adds long-context documents, where defenses that look strong on short inputs can fail [2608.28411]; PIArena and pikit provide platforms and toolkits that vary attacks, delivery and defenses [2604.08499; 2609.36817]. We use environment-state scoring as a design principle, InjecAgent for the external test, and participant-submitted LLMail-Inject items for calibration only.

**Defenses.** Prompt-level defenses such as spotlighting, which transforms untrusted input to signal its provenance, have been proposed [2403.14720]. Capability-based designs such as CaMeL explicitly separate trusted-query control/data flow from untrusted content [2503.18813]; Progent enforces least-privilege rules at the tool-call boundary [2504.11703]; MELON and Task Shield check tool calls against a re-execution or the user's goal [2502.05174; 2412.16682]. A study reports that output filtering resisted a long adaptive campaign targeting system-prompt leakage [2604.23887].

**Adaptive attacks.** Defenses that look strong on static sets are broken by defense-aware attackers: adaptive attacks bypass all eight indirect-injection defenses the authors evaluate, with attack success above 50% [2503.00061], a multi-lab study breaks a dozen defenses with search, reinforcement learning and human red-teaming [2510.09023], and a black-box method generates adaptive injections against ten defenses, where static benchmarks overestimate their efficacy [2606.15057]. Automated attacks adapted to AgentDojo are model-dependent [2606.10525], and a protocol for adaptive evaluation of out-of-band defenses has been specified [2606.26479]. We ran no adaptive attacker.

**Evaluation validity.** This is the closest line of work. Shaw audits an agent-security harness and identifies payload non-delivery and tool-identity scoring as defects; scoring by tool identity overstates success relative to argument-level scoring [2609.32691]. Sakib et al. place a byte-identical payload in a tool output or in a tool description across 13 models and four AgentDojo suites, find that many model pairs change their relative ordering, and find that mitigations effective against tool-output attacks can leave exposure through tool descriptions [2605.30454]. Akinrele and Gowda show that detector rankings depend on the evaluation regime and the false-positive budget [2605.26999]. Pathade et al. decompose attack success rate into six axes (A1 Unit of Analysis, A2 Success Oracle, A3 Trials and Non-determinism, A4 Attacker Adaptivity, A5 Attacker Knowledge of the Defense, A6 Binarization of Partial Success), meta-analyze agentic-security papers, give a ten-item reporting checklist, and show analytically how large an effect a benchmark of modest size can detect [2609.25173]. Their contribution is analytical and meta-analytic, and in our reading they report no defense experiment. A public red-teaming arena reports attack-success rates ranging from 0.5% to 8.5% across 13 frontier models [2603.15714].

**Position.** We do not propose a defense or a benchmark. Pathade et al.'s axes cover general agentic security evaluation design. Four of our checks lie outside those axes, which the authors state are not exhaustive: blocked-payload labeling (M2), scenario validity (M3), attack-set authorship independence (M4) and defense-channel verification (M6). Two of our checks refine existing axes on our traces: the success endpoint (M1) refines A2, and per-model reporting with a noise floor (M5) refines A3. Our evidence is a single case study on one testbed. Only M6 is left as a proposal (§4.7).
