### OLD abstract (250 words, wc -w)
Tool-using LLM agent defenses are usually compared by attack success rate, whose measurement choices are unreported. We ask whether public attack sets yield measurable undefended rates on five 2026 targets. One target is measurable on InjecAgent; the others are below-threshold or undetermined, so this panel does not establish a general low-ASR floor. The case study uses one testbed: a tool harness with an executed-call endpoint, an exploratory two-scenario run on three open-weight models, a partially independent 168-episode attack set, an external InjecAgent test on two models, and calibration of five 2026 models.

The success endpoint changes the verdict. A static tool policy executes 0/72 attacks but is invisible to proposal-level scoring (65/72 proposed), and lowers benign utility from 45/45 to 15/45. Under the strict rule, the exploratory run has no valid attack, so its complete stop is not evidence. On the partially independent set, which passes the rule, neither detector-style defense shows change beyond replicate noise (B3: 56/167 undefended against 57/167 defended; CORE: 57/168 against 57/168), and susceptibility differs by model (41/56 against 8/56). On two selected targets, one of which (qwen3.8-flash, n = 36) fails the n ≥ 40 rule, proposal-level attacker-tool-call rates were about ten points lower with a tool-channel delimiter; this selected, unregistered, protocol-deviating comparison is exploratory only. Traces illustrate six candidate checks: M3 is a post hoc validity finding; M5 is supported by descriptive data; M1, M2 and M4 are illustrations; M6 is proposed. None is prospectively validated. We claim no general defense effectiveness or ineffectiveness.

### NEW abstract (249 words, wc -w)
LLM-agent defenses are compared by attack success rate under unreported measurement choices. Of five 2026 targets, only one yields a measurable undefended rate on public attack sets (InjecAgent); the others are below-threshold or undetermined, so the panel establishes no general low-ASR floor. One testbed is used: a tool harness with an executed-call endpoint, an exploratory two-scenario run (three open-weight models), a partially independent 168-episode set, an external InjecAgent test on two models, and the calibration.

The success endpoint changes the verdict. A static tool policy executes 0/72 attacks but is invisible to proposal-level scoring (65/72 proposed), and lowers benign utility from 45/45 to 15/45. Under the strict rule the exploratory run has no valid attack; its complete stop is not evidence. On the valid, partially independent set, neither detector-style defense changes executed attacks (B3: 56/167 undefended against 57/167 defended; CORE: 57/168 against 57/168), but neither acted on the injected content (one took no action; the other only removed the user message's final period), so this comparison is an identical-input repeat, not a test of detector-style defenses. Susceptibility differs by model (41/56 against 8/56). On two selected targets (one, qwen3.8-flash, n = 36, fails the n ≥ 40 rule), a tool-channel delimiter lowered proposal-level attacker-tool-call rates by about ten points; this selected, unregistered, protocol-deviating comparison is exploratory. Of six candidate checks, M3 is a post hoc validity finding, M5 descriptively supported, M1, M2 and M4 illustrations, M6 proposed; none is prospectively validated. We claim no general defense effectiveness or ineffectiveness.
